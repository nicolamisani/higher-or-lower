# Servings — for review

The text students see under each food name, beside its weight. Written for
readers whose English is a second language: common words, a vessel or a count
wherever one exists, no idioms.

## Meat and fish

The first version used the UK dietary-guideline portion, 90 g of meat. That is
not a dinner: a restaurant steak is over 200 g and an Italian *secondo* is
usually 100 to 150 g. These are what somebody actually eats.

Three of the nine are not main courses and are not pretended to be. Ham is a
sandwich, prawns were a starter, canned tuna is a salad. Forcing them all to
dinner size would invent meals nobody has — and the fact that the service
differs is the point of a functional unit, not a flaw in it.

## Beans, nuts and soya

| food | serving shown | weight | g CO₂e |
|---|---|---|---|

**Dry weights now say so in the weight itself** — `100 g dry` for lentils,
`75 g dry` for rice and pasta. The description stays plain and nothing is
hidden. That closes the question left open earlier.

**Almonds: a small bowl, 40 nuts, 50 g.** A kernel is about 1.25 g. The
dietary "handful" is 30 g, but a bowl of nuts beside a drink is nearer 50, and
that is what somebody actually eats in one sitting.

A portion between those two, 38 to 40 g, had to be avoided: almonds sit next to
breakfast cereal in the chain at 102, so the comparison would have been decided
by about 2 per cent — a coin flip rather than a judgement. Both the handful and
the bowl give a clean call either way, so choosing between *equally honest*
options on how well they play is legitimate. Choosing a portion to produce a
particular answer would not be.

At 50 g almonds pass rice, pasta and potatoes and sit just under a glass of
orange juice. **A bowl of nuts outweighing a plate of pasta** is a better fact
than the one it replaces.

### What this does to potatoes against tofu, one of the six lecture pairs

100 g of tofu was a small main — about 8 g of protein. 150 g, half a standard
block, is what somebody actually cooks. The honest consequence:

| | g CO₂e |
|---|---|
| Potatoes, 180 g | 142 |
| Tofu, 150 g | 150 |

So the pair stops being a reversal and becomes a **near-tie, 1.06×**. The slide
answer is no longer counter-intuitive — and that is the right outcome, because
the alternative was choosing a portion to produce the answer we preferred,
which is exactly what this game trains students to catch. Worth saying out loud
in the debrief: two cheap plant foods land in the same place, and what separates
them is how much of each you put on the plate.

Half a small zucchini was never a serving; a medium one is 200 g and people
cook the whole thing. A tomato is 120 g, not 80 — that was a small one.

**"1 potato" was the ambiguous one**, because a potato runs from 100 g to
300 g, so it reads "1 large potato" at 200 g. I avoided "1 baked potato",
which is more vivid but implies an oven, and the cooking is not in the figure.

**Broccoli** is "a third of a head" rather than "5 pieces", because a head is a
fixed thing (about 350 g) and a piece is not.

A real 200 g potato serving comes to 158 against tofu's 150, so **potatoes
against tofu returns to the counter-intuitive answer** — this time because both
portions are true, not because either was chosen to produce it.

### Why the skin is no longer mentioned

The first version said "without the skin", which implied that the peel's
emissions had been taken out. They have not, and they cannot be. In AGRIBALYSE,
`Apple, pulp, raw`, `Apple, pulp and peel, raw` and `Apple, var. Golden, pulp
and skin, raw` are **all 0.408** — the same number. The word "pulp" comes from
the French food-composition database and describes which part you *eat*; it is
not an allocation of impact. There is one figure for the fruit as grown and the
skin is inside it.

So these are weights **as bought**, skin on, and the labels say nothing about
peeling. Nobody measured the peel separately, and pretending otherwise would
teach a split that does not exist.

Worth keeping for the debrief: this is the same problem as the beef cuts. When
one process yields something you eat and something you throw away, a person has
to decide where the emissions land. Here the convention puts all of them on the
kilogram of fruit.

**One whole avocado is 310 g CO₂e** — more than a bottle of beer.

## "The X in Y" — a deliberate wording, not a clumsy one

Five foods are normally eaten with something else, and five labels now say
**the ham in a sandwich**, **the tuna in a salad**, **the butter on 2 slices of
bread**, **the peanut butter on 1 slice of bread**, **the oil on one salad** —
alongside **the gin in one gin and tonic**, which set the pattern.

The figure covers that food alone. The bread, the salad leaves and the tonic
are **not** counted, and neither is any cooking: the AGRIBALYSE rows used here
are the raw, as-bought product, farm to shop.

**The game does not say this anywhere, on purpose.** A student who notices that
the bread is missing has found the system boundary without being told, which is
the third of the five questions on slide 13. Writing it on the end card would
hand over the one thing worth discovering. Do not add it later thinking it was
an oversight.

## Still open

**Gin.** You suggested "half a cocktail glass". A cocktail glass holds about
150 ml, so half of one would be 75 ml — three times the measure the figure is
based on. The table uses "the gin in one gin and tonic", which is the standard
25 ml. If you prefer the glass image, the honest version is "1 shot glass".

**Coffee.** The weight now reads `7 g ground`, which is one espresso. A filter
or American coffee uses two to three times that, so its footprint is two to
three times higher. Worth a word on screen, or leave it?

- Rice, pasta and lentils: the weight says `dry`, so the description can stay plain.
- Almonds: a small bowl, 40 nuts, 50 g.
- Meat and fish: real serving sizes, not dietary-guideline ones.
- Dairy: a whole mozzarella ball, two eggs, a breakfast pot of yogurt.
- Tofu: half a block. At a true 200 g potato serving the pair goes back to
  potatoes anyway, so nothing was lost.
- Vegetables: real servings — a whole zucchini, a large potato, a 120 g tomato.
- Fruit: weighed as bought, skin on, because the peel cannot be separated.
- Biscuits: 4 with breakfast, 50 g. 25 g was two small ones, and it had made
  biscuits against peanut butter a 1.11x coin flip.
- Crisps: 1 big bag, 100 g. A 30 g bag is the single-serve size sold at a bar,
  not what one person opens at home.

### One reorder needed when this is applied

With these portions, the chain would run HIGHER five times in a row around
Lentils → Potatoes → Tofu → Egg → Chicken → Pork chop. Moving **Lentils to sit
just after Tofu** fixes it: the balance returns to exactly 25 higher / 25 lower,
the longest run of one answer drops to 3, and all six lecture pairs stay
adjacent. With the almond bowl included the final chain is **26 higher / 24
lower, longest run 3**.

## Words I removed from the first version

"a mugful", "a fistful", "a hand long", "the size of a matchbox", "egg-sized",
"a heaped teaspoon", "spears", "florets". All of them are either British
idioms, cooking jargon, or units that do not travel.


## The running order, re-solved

The portions above changed enough numbers that the chain had to be rebuilt. The
constraints:

- **Carrot opens and the flown beans close.** The first call is free confidence,
  the last is the lesson.
- **The six lecture pairs stay adjacent** — carrots/beef, gin/wine, beer/milk,
  biscuits/peanut butter, potatoes/tofu, ham/tuna.
- **No comparison decided by less than 1.30x.** Tuna against salmon was 1.01 and
  rice against pasta 1.02; both pairs are now far apart in the deck.
- **No answer repeated more than three times** running, so no fixed strategy pays.
- **No food group three times in a row**, which is what made the old pork block
  dull.

Result: **24 higher / 26 lower, longest run of one answer 3, longest run of one
food group 2, and exactly one comparison under 1.30x** — potatoes against tofu
at 1.05, which is unavoidable because it is one of the lecture pairs and the two
genuinely land in the same place. Worth naming in the debrief as a real tie
rather than hiding it.

A purely machine-optimised order was tried first and discarded. It satisfied
every constraint and destroyed the teaching: the explanation lines refer to the
food before them ("the same pig again", "wetter than cheddar", "a little heavier
than the beans"), and a random walk gives a student no way to build a theory.
The order in the game is a hand-written thematic sequence that the search was
only allowed to repair, not replace.
